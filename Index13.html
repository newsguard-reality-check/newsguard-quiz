How to Spot Measles Misinformation
  • Go to public health authorities first. The CDC, WHO, and your state or local health department publish current case counts and vaccine guidance — treat those as the baseline, not a viral post.
  • Be skeptical of "natural immunity" framing. Claims that catching a disease is healthier than being vaccinated against it are a recurring myth pattern — check what peer-reviewed research actually says, not just what sounds intuitive.
  • Watch for misattributed research. A claim that a respected institution "found" or "proved" something dramatic deserves a direct check of that institution's own publications before you believe or share it.
  • Question miracle prevention or cures. Vitamins, supplements, and home remedies can support health, but be wary of any claim that one of them prevents or cures a specific infectious disease.
  • Look for scientific consensus, not outliers. A single contrarian claim circulating online rarely overturns decades of consistent findings from major health agencies worldwide.
  • Check loaded or shocking language. Claims built around emotionally charged phrasing (like ingredient scares) are often designed to provoke a reaction before you can verify them — look up the actual ingredient list from the manufacturer or CDC.
